      - explain: 'The question is **leading**: "Do you agree …" pushes people towards saying yes.'

## Why this matters

Almost every news story that begins *"a survey found …"* is based on a sample. Whether
you can trust it depends on who was asked, how many were asked and what exactly they were
asked. Being able to judge that is one of the most useful skills in the whole statistics
course.

## Choosing how much data

| | Small sample | Large sample |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Advantage** | Quick and cheap to collect | More reliable, closer to the truth |
| **Disadvantage** | Less reliable; one odd result has a big effect | Slow and expensive |

You also need to decide the **degree of accuracy** — for example, rounding amounts spent to
the nearest dollar, or times to the nearest minute.

## How bias gets in

**Intentional bias** — choosing data to give the picture you want. An advert that quotes
only its happiest customers is biased on purpose.

**Unintentional bias** — an accident of how you sampled. Asking the first 50 people into a
cinema about their favourite film seems fair, but if 38 of them are men, the results lean
towards men's opinions.

## Writing good questions

| Problem | Example | Better |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leading question | *"Don't you agree that …?"* | *"What do you think about …?"* |
| Vague options | *"How much exercise do you do?"* | Tick boxes: *0–1 h / 1–3 h / 3–5 h / 5+ h per week* |
| Personal or awkward | *"How old are you?"* | Age ranges: *under 18 / 18–30 / 31–50 / over 50* |
| Asks for data you don't need | Name, address … | Only ask what answers your question |

> **Run a pilot survey.** Try your questionnaire on a few people first. Answers like *"?"*,
> *"half"* or a dash show you which questions need rewriting.
