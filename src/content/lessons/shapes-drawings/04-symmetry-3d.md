## Why this matters

Symmetry in 3D is everywhere in design and engineering: car bodies, aircraft, bottles and
buildings are often built to be symmetrical, because symmetrical objects balance, look
right and are easier to manufacture in matching halves.

## From lines to planes

In 2D, a rectangle has **two lines** of symmetry. Stretch that rectangle back into a
cuboid, and each line becomes a **plane** running through the shape — plus one more that
cuts across the middle. That is why a cuboid has three planes of symmetry.

## Counting planes for prisms

| Prism (cross-section) | Lines in the cross-section | Planes of symmetry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equilateral triangle | 3 | 4 |
| Isosceles triangle | 1 | 2 |
| Scalene triangle | 0 | 1 |
| Regular pentagon | 5 | 6 |
| Regular hexagon | 6 | 7 |
| Semicircle | 1 | 2 |
| Circle (a cylinder) | infinitely many | infinitely many |

Notice that every prism has **at least one** plane of symmetry — the one halfway along.

## The cube is a special case

A square prism that is *not* a cube has $4 + 1 = 5$ planes. But when the length matches the
square's side, the shape looks the same from every face, and you get extra diagonal planes.
A cube has:

- 3 planes parallel to its faces, and
- 6 planes that slice diagonally through a pair of opposite edges,

making **9** in total.

> **Drawing tip.** On isometric paper, shade one plane per copy of the shape. It is far
> clearer than trying to draw several planes on a single diagram.
